The problem

Three reasons your current tool sits unused.

01

It was built for software jobs

Most resume tools are built around tech jobs and tech keywords. The people you serve are more often nurses, electricians, CDL drivers, teachers, caseworkers and warehouse leads. Advice written for engineers tells a welder to measure their "impact" and nothing useful comes out.

02

Buying stalls on the data question

Any tool that uploads a resume is holding names, addresses, phone numbers and job history for people who may be under 18, veterans, or coming out of prison. That means a security review, a data agreement and a retention policy. Most pilots die right there.

03

Nobody can explain the score

A tool that says 62 out of 100 and will not say why is no use in a session. Advisors cannot build a lesson around it, and the person cannot fix anything without paying to see what is wrong.

Data handling

There is no server to review.

The whole tool, every word list and every scoring rule, is inside the web page itself. The work happens on the person's own computer. The server sends one file and receives nothing back.

You do not have to take our word for it. Your own IT staff can check it in about a minute. Open the browser network tab and run a resume through it.

It also means most of the paperwork does not apply. We never handle personal data, so there is nothing for a data agreement to cover, no retention schedule to write, and no breach to plan for.

What that means on paper

The questions a security review asks, and the straight answers.

  • Where is participant data stored? On their own device only, in browser storage they can wipe with one button.
  • What do you retain? A count of page visits per day, and nothing else. No database of people, no accounts.

  • Can we audit the code? Yes, all of it, in public. That is what the license is for.

Procurement questions

Straight answers, including what is missing.

What happens to a participant's resume?

It stays in the browser tab. It is read by JavaScript already on their machine, scored there, and never sent anywhere. If they save a version, it goes to that browser's local storage on that device, and one button deletes all of it. Close the tab without saving and nothing is left anywhere.

The server counts how many times each page was opened, so we can answer how many people used it. That count is a number per day. It holds no IP address, no browser details, no cookie and no resume text, and the page never sends anything to it. You can read the whole thing at /stats.

Do we need a data processing agreement?

Almost certainly not, because we never handle the data. Nothing is handled on a server, nothing is stored, and no outside company is involved. We are not lawyers and your counsel should check it against your own policy, but there is usually nothing for the agreement to cover.

Is it accessible?

It is built to be. You can use the whole thing with a keyboard. Headings and landmarks are marked up properly, tab controls carry ARIA roles, focus outlines are visible, and it honors reduced motion settings. Every piece of text has been checked against WCAG AA contrast in both light and dark modes. It has not been checked by an outside accessibility firm. If you need a VPAT, tell us early and we will sort out how to get one.

Does it integrate with our SSO or student system?

Not today. There is no account system to attach an identity to, which is the same choice that removes the privacy problem. If you self-host, you can put it behind your existing login at the network level. Closer integration is work we would have to do, and worth talking about if you really need it.

What reporting do we get?

Honestly, very little right now, and for the same reason: with no server there is nothing collecting usage. Summary reporting that does not break the privacy promise is the next thing being built, and pilot partners get to say what it should measure. If funder reporting is a hard requirement today, say so before you commit.

Who is behind this, and what if you disappear?

It is built and looked after by one person. That is a fair question about any small company. The license is the answer: it is open source under AGPL-3.0, so the whole working product is public and yours to keep running. Self-host it and nothing that happens to us can take it away from the people you serve.

Is the score the same one an employer's system produces?

No, and be careful with anyone who says otherwise. Workday, Greenhouse, Taleo and iCIMS all rank differently and none publish their formula. What this scores is the set of things all of them are known to check, plus the writing standards a human applies afterwards. It is practice, not a final answer, and the rubric says so on the page.

How do we evaluate it without a procurement process?

Just open it and use it. There is no signup, no trial and no sales call, so an advisor can try it on a real job posting in five minutes and tell you if it is worth a meeting.
